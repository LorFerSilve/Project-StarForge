# Visual Direction

> **Status:** Design Complete  
> **Authority:** Project-wide visual identity, realism/stylization target, shape language, material readability, lighting philosophy, color semantics, camera readability, and visual hierarchy

## 1. Purpose

Project StarForge must look coherent enough that a player can identify function, danger, ownership, and technological character before reading UI text.

The visual target is **stylized grounded science fiction**.

## 2. Stylization Target

The game does not pursue photorealism.

It uses believable engineering forms, materials, wear, lighting, scale, and mechanical function while simplifying detail to preserve:

- readability;
- strong silhouettes;
- achievable asset scope;
- stable visual identity;
- performance headroom for systemic scenes.

## 3. Core Visual Pillars

1. **Functional:** important geometry should look usable and mechanically purposeful.
2. **Readable:** interactable and dangerous states must remain legible in motion.
3. **Modular:** station/ship construction uses visually compatible modular language.
4. **Lived-In:** human spaces show repair, reuse, labels, storage, wear, and adaptation.
5. **Cosmic Contrast:** tight industrial interiors contrast with large, quiet space vistas.
6. **Faction Distinction:** factions differ through form/material/layout/signage, not color alone.

## 4. Shape Language

Player/Horizon technology primarily uses:

- rectilinear structural frames;
- chamfered corners;
- exposed service access;
- modular panel seams;
- visible conduits where useful;
- readable mechanical joints.

Decorative surface noise must not obscure functional boundaries.

## 5. Scale Language

Doors, ladders, consoles, modules, corridors, cargo, and machinery must communicate believable human scale.

Large systems such as reactors and hangars should feel materially larger than ordinary room props rather than merely using UI labels to communicate importance.

## 6. Material Language

Baseline material families:

- painted structural alloy;
- exposed metal;
- composite armor;
- glass/transparent pressure surfaces;
- polymer/rubber utility surfaces;
- insulation;
- ceramic/high-temperature components;
- emissive displays/indicators.

Material identity should remain distinguishable under neutral lighting.

## 7. Wear and Damage

Wear is localized according to use:

- handles/edges polish;
- floor paths scuff;
- industrial zones accumulate grime;
- heat zones discolor;
- repaired areas show patches/replacement panels;
- impact damage follows actual damage locations.

Global random dirt overlays are not the primary wear solution.

## 8. Damage Readability

Damage presentation can use:

- deformation where authored;
- broken panels;
- sparks/arcing;
- smoke/vapor;
- exposed wiring;
- scorch marks;
- pressure leaks;
- disabled lighting;
- warning indicators.

Visual damage must not claim a gameplay state that has not occurred.

## 9. Lighting Philosophy

Lighting supports both atmosphere and function.

Primary rules:

- navigation-critical spaces remain readable;
- working areas have task-oriented illumination;
- emergency states can alter lighting but never make mandatory interaction unreadable;
- space exteriors use strong directional contrast and local work lights;
- emissive surfaces do not replace actual visibility where gameplay requires it.

## 10. Darkness

Darkness may create tension and make flashlights/sensors useful.

However:

- mandatory UI text remains readable;
- critical interaction targets have a discoverable feedback path;
- accessibility options can raise gameplay visibility without changing world rules.

## 11. Color Philosophy

Color reinforces function but is never the sole carrier of required information.

Canonical semantic families:

- **Neutral:** structural/general information;
- **Cool Cyan/Blue:** player/Horizon operational/interactive information;
- **Green:** confirmed safe/success/healthy state;
- **Amber:** caution/degraded/attention;
- **Red:** critical danger/hostile/failed state;
- **Violet/Magenta:** anomalous/Fracture-related phenomena.

Icons, shapes, labels, animation, or audio must accompany critical color states.

## 12. Horizon Identity

Horizon evolves visually from damaged pre-Fracture industrial infrastructure into a repaired, customized, inhabited command station.

Growth is visible through:

- repaired surfaces;
- added modules;
- routed infrastructure;
- inhabited workspaces;
- crew/robot activity;
- improved lighting/signage;
- defenses and docking activity.

Progression must not simply swap the station to a higher-tier texture set.

## 13. Player Equipment

Player gear reads as practical expedition equipment.

Silhouette communicates:

- environmental protection;
- backpack/cargo burden;
- equipped weapon/tool class;
- suit capability modules where externally visible.

## 14. Robot Readability

Robot classes use distinct silhouettes and tool profiles.

Examples:

- Recon: small/light sensor-forward form;
- Repair: visible manipulators/tool package;
- Shield: projector hardware;
- Breacher/Demolition: specialized front/tool/payload geometry;
- Heavy Mech: unmistakably large armored footprint.

A player should not need a floating class label to tell a Heavy Mech from a Repair Drone.

## 15. Enemy/Faction Readability

Faction allegiance cannot depend only on a colored outline.

It should be readable through:

- silhouette;
- equipment construction;
- insignia;
- architecture;
- uniforms/robot form;
- material treatment;
- signage.

HUD identification can confirm ambiguous cases.

## 16. Fracture/Anomaly Visual Language

Anomaly phenomena use a distinct language:

- spatially implausible but bounded distortions;
- violet/magenta spectral accents;
- nonstandard light behavior;
- ordered geometric interference patterns;
- localized material/electrical reactions.

They should look physically consequential rather than magical fantasy effects.

## 17. Space Visuals

Space prioritizes depth and navigation readability.

Use:

- strong silhouettes;
- star/background contrast;
- navigation lights;
- thruster emissions;
- distant celestial scale cues;
- restrained particulate effects.

Dense decorative space fog is not the default.

## 18. Visual Hierarchy

During active gameplay the visual priority order is:

1. immediate lethal hazard/threat;
2. direct interaction target;
3. current objective/path-critical information;
4. allies/robots and tactical state;
5. environmental storytelling/detail.

Presentation may shift this hierarchy contextually.

## 19. Outline Policy

Constant bright outlines around all interactive objects are not baseline.

Contextual highlighting is allowed when:

- player explicitly scans;
- crosshair/focus targets an interactable;
- accessibility setting requests stronger highlighting;
- mission/objective state requires it.

## 20. Camera Readability

First-person presentation avoids unnecessary camera motion that competes with aiming/traversal.

Head bob, weapon sway, hit shake, explosions, and ship camera shake must be bounded and separately reducible through accessibility settings.

## 21. Spacecraft View

Third-person ship view prioritizes:

- ship silhouette;
- velocity/orientation understanding;
- collision awareness;
- target readability;
- weapon firing arcs/feedback.

Cockpit view uses the same gameplay information, with cockpit surfaces supplementing rather than withholding required state.

## 22. Effects Restraint

Bloom, lens effects, chromatic distortion, particles, sparks, smoke, and screen overlays are tools, not default decoration.

They must not obscure:

- crosshair;
- hazards;
- doors;
- targets;
- critical warnings.

## 23. Text in World

World labels use concise industrial language:

- POWER;
- AIRLOCK;
- REACTOR;
- DOCK 02;
- LIFE SUPPORT;
- DECK B.

Critical world signage combines text with shape/symbol where practical.

## 24. Readability at Distance

Important objects use silhouette/scale/lighting differences before tiny texture labels.

Gameplay identification cannot require reading microscopic decals at combat distance.

## 25. Explicit Non-Goals

The baseline does not require:

- photorealism;
- cinematic film grain;
- constant object outlines;
- color-only state communication;
- excessive surface greebling;
- permanent dramatic darkness;
- unrestricted visual destruction;
- high-amplitude camera shake.

## 26. Tuneable Parameters

Exact palette values, exposure, contrast, bloom amount, decal density, wear intensity, effect opacity, camera shake, and highlight strength are tuneable within the fixed visual principles.

## 27. Dependencies

References GDS-1 camera/scope rules and all physical gameplay domains. Detailed environment/station/faction/UI/VFX specifications refine this direction.

## 28. Open Questions

None in the project-wide visual-direction baseline.
